# Parola course redesign from absolute beginner to C2

Draft for discussion, 30 September 2026. This is a curriculum, product, assessment, and execution plan. It does not change the application or authorize deployment. The goal is a coherent learning path from no Italian to advanced, flexible use, with the existing word and verb lessons supporting that path.

The course must teach the language required for an activity before judging a learner's use of it. Every activity will declare its target, prerequisite knowledge, vocabulary support, and the evidence it can legitimately produce. This is the central correction to the current course.

**The scope includes the full range of language use needed on the path to C2.** Grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation, listening, reading, writing, spoken interaction, and explaining information to someone else all need planned development. Each has its own evidence; completing grammar exercises cannot silently stand for listening or speaking proficiency. Course completion and a learner's demonstrated skill profile will be distinct. The product should support preparation toward C2 without claiming that a completion badge is an accredited CEFR assessment.

The [CEFR Companion Volume](https://rm.coe.int/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages-learning-teaching/16809ea0d4) supplies the framework of reception, production, interaction, and mediation, including Pre-A1. The [Italian Profile](https://www.unistrapg.it/profilo_lingua_italiana/site/index.html) provides an Italian-specific calibration resource for A1–B2. These inform coverage; the unit ordering, examples, and learning policies below are proposed editorial decisions for Parola, not an official prescribed syllabus or copied course.

**The approved product direction remains the foundation.** Keep Resume at the top of Learn, the My Course selector, direct access to individual words and verb cases, the existing Review area and Verb Lab, and the application's visual language. The main course develops language use in context; word lessons remain short and mainly recognition/matching; verb lessons retain focused teaching and practice for the selected case. Learners choose whether to continue after a lesson or case, can pause or save for later, and can browse without compulsory locks.

The five existing core verb cases remain present, passato prossimo, imperfetto, future, and present conditional. Appropriate present and past progressive work stays connected to present and imperfetto. Formal Lei remains integrated with ordinary examples and contrasts. Advanced cases remain available individually and become recommended when the course teaches them. A grammar activity using one form does not complete a whole verb case. Existing full-case requirements in [TAUGHT-LESSON-SPEC.md](TAUGHT-LESSON-SPEC.md) remain in force unless explicitly revised.

**Comprehensive coverage will be documented as a dependency map before bulk authoring.** The hierarchy is course → level → practical unit → short lesson → teachable targets → authored steps and checks. A unit has a meaningful task, such as introducing yourself, resolving a booking problem, or synthesizing differing accounts. Its lesson count follows the concepts involved; no fixed two-card/four-question template applies.

Each target must have the following coverage record:

| Record | Required detail |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose | What the learner can understand or do, and in which context. |
| Language | The exact form, meaning, sound, construction, word sense, register, or discourse choice being taught. |
| Prerequisites | Necessary target IDs and lexical knowledge; whether each is required, supplied, or briefly refreshed. |
| Introduction | The specific earlier step that models and explains it. |
| Practice | Supported examples, independent checks, and meaningful variation. |
| Contrast | Likely confusions and examples that actually distinguish them. |
| Transfer | Where it reappears with different familiar vocabulary or a new practical purpose. |
| Retention | Later retrieval opportunities and the review task family. |
| Assessment | What is graded automatically, what is reflected on, and what needs a rubric or external feedback. |
| Review status | Linguistic source, author, independent reviewer, and known limitations. |

There will be two maps: a concept map for grammar and communication, and a lexical map of the words and constructions that make each activity accessible. Dependency checks must work on every actual route through a lesson, including error repair, skipped steps, and advanced entry. The app can offer a small bridge where prerequisites are missing, or supply background forms while assessing only the new target. A learner entering B2 is not forced through all of A1, but receives support where needed.

**The level sequence will develop practical abilities with increasing independence.** Foundations are an entry ramp within Everyday Italian, not an extra certification level. The outline below defines the curriculum's intended coverage; the subsequent authoring inventory will break each unit into individually validated lessons.

The level goals below are brief orientation points informed by the [CEFR global scale](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/table-1-cefr-3.3-common-reference-levels-global-scale). The detailed unit tables are original proposed teaching sequences. They will be cross-checked against Italian-specific inventories and representative learner performances, not treated as a certification specification.

| Stage | Intended development | Example integrated outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Foundations | First contact with meaning, sound, useful chunks, and how to ask for help. | Participate in a supported greeting and introduction. |
| A1 | Basic personal information, familiar things, simple needs and everyday exchanges. | Introduce yourself, ask a simple question, and complete a supported transaction. |
| A2 | Routine interaction, simple accounts, plans and descriptions. | Arrange an outing, describe a past event, and handle a small change. |
| B1 | Connected familiar communication and greater everyday independence. | Explain what happened, give reasons, and respond to a practical problem. |
| B2 | Sustained viewpoints, comparison, negotiation, and more complex input. | Compare alternatives, support a position, and respond to an objection. |
| C1 | Flexible complex communication, implicit meaning and organized discourse. | Produce and discuss a reasoned brief drawing on different sources. |
| C2 | Precise synthesis, nuance, interpretation, and flexible communication across demanding situations. | Reconcile differing accounts and communicate accurately to different audiences. |

Foundations contain a compact sequence of supported exchanges. They do not delay useful communication until a learner has memorized an alphabet or a conjugation table. A few phrases are deliberately learned as whole expressions; their grammar is unpacked in A1 and A2.

| Foundations unit | Purpose and language work |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Make first contact | Greet and leave, give a name, and ask a name; model familiar and polite address in short exchanges. |
| 2. Keep the exchange going | Thank someone, get attention, ask for repetition, and say that something was not understood. |
| 3. Connect familiar meaning and sound | Use supported identity/place frames, hear and read known phrases, and recognize how audio, meaning and written Italian fit together. |

A1 grows from those exchanges into simple reusable sentence patterns. Article variants, plurals and agreement are taught in separate steps. Small pronunciation lessons are woven through the units using introduced words: vowels and familiar-word stress, c/ch, g/gh, sc/sch, gn/gli, silent h and spelling cues, consonant length, written accents, and question intonation. Each contrast is introduced before it is assessed; hearing a word once is not assumed to establish its spelling.

| A1 unit | Purpose and language work |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Say who you are | Unpack sono, sei and è, then plural forms when needed; introduce yourself and someone else, origin, pronoun omission and formal Lei. |
| 2. Name familiar things | Introduce noun meanings with article and gender; teach il/la and un/una, then l’, lo/uno and feminine elision in manageable steps. |
| 3. Say what you have | Build avere through familiar possessions; develop numbers, age, hunger and thirst after their words and forms are introduced. |
| 4. Describe one or several things | Teach noun plurals, then plural articles and adjective agreement; include frequent exceptions and invariant forms in useful contexts. |
| 5. Talk about everyday actions | Build regular -are present forms across persons, simple questions and non; use familiar daily activities. |
| 6. Expand everyday actions | Teach -ere, -ire and -isc patterns through separate model verbs before transfer; develop food, study and home routines. |
| 7. Go places and do things | Introduce selected andare, fare and venire forms; teach a/in/da/con in the specific constructions used. |
| 8. Find things and places | Teach c’è/ci sono, location and simple directions; add frequent preposition/article combinations after both components. |
| 9. Say whose and which | Develop possessives, selected family expressions, questo and quello through familiar objects and people. |
| 10. Buy and arrange things | Develop prices, time, days, amounts and count/mass contrasts in separate short lessons; use a shop or café exchange. |
| 11. Ask, respond and express preferences | Teach piacere and selected modal patterns, polite requests, familiar commands and simple links; revisit tu/Lei throughout. |
| 12. Describe a first completed event | Introduce a small useful passato prossimo set with avere, then selected essere examples with explicit agreement; send a short message about yesterday. |

The A1 integrated task combines an introduction, a familiar transaction, a short sign/message, and a simple account using only taught patterns. Present-progressive language can be accessed in the existing verb lessons with a short stare bridge; systematic course contrasts are developed in A2. A1 never treats the simple present as exclusively “right now.”

A2 develops routine independence and short connected accounts. The required progression is to teach the component forms, then their construction, then a meaningful contrast. Full selected verb-case practice stays available through the verb lessons; the course revisits those forms in communication.

| A2 unit | Purpose and language work |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Describe your routine | Unpack familiar reflexive chunks, teach the pronouns and present forms through daily activities. |
| 2. Describe finished events | Build regular and common irregular participles with avere; use a diary entry or completed errand. |
| 3. Describe movement and reflexive events | Teach selected essere verbs, agreement and reflexive past constructions; model whole forms before recall. |
| 4. Describe habits and background | Teach imperfetto families and frequent irregular forms separately; describe childhood, weather and past circumstances. |
| 5. Put events into a story | Contrast background, habit and bounded events, then interrupted actions, using coherent sequences. |
| 6. Focus on an action underway | Introduce the needed stare forms before sto/stavo plus gerund; distinguish ongoing focus from routine, states and completed events. |
| 7. Refer to known objects | Teach lo/la/li/le with familiar nouns and clear reference; practise comprehension before unsupported selection. |
| 8. Say to whom and place pronouns | Introduce recipient pronouns, then finite-verb and infinitive placement; explicitly model attached forms and alternatives. |
| 9. Make future plans | Teach regular and common irregular future forms, then compare future with natural present-tense plans. |
| 10. Request, invite and respond politely | Develop conditional patterns, wishes, invitations, apologies and formal address; introduce needed stems and pronoun combinations before production. |
| 11. Compare and estimate | Teach comparison, selected superlatives, approximate amounts and quantifiers with count/mass agreement. |
| 12. Give and understand instructions | Stage tu, voi and Lei commands and their negatives; keep person, intention and politeness explicit. |
| 13. Explain source, place and duration | Develop da, governed prepositions and useful contractions through appointments, services and continuing situations. |
| 14. Connect useful information | Link sequence, cause and result; introduce simple relative che and open conditions with taught forms; prepare a short invitation or account. |

The A2 integrated task combines arrangements, a brief past account and a change of plan across a short dialogue and message. Later retrieval reuses the same core distinctions in different familiar settings. Complex clitic combinations, sophisticated hypotheticals and advanced progressive restrictions receive separate bridges at later stages; they are not hidden prerequisites for an A2 question.

B1 consolidates forms into connected communication. A2 past contrast, common pronouns, questions, future and conditional provide its entry foundations; missing components get short bridges. Frequent subjunctive expressions can be introduced as limited frames before broad productive control is expected. The [CILS guidelines](https://cils.unistrasi.it/public/articoli/76/linee_guida_cils.pdf) provide one Italian calibration reference for the staged expansion of grammatical and communicative demands. They do not determine a mandatory ordering for every learner.

| B1 unit | Purpose and language work |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Tell what happened | Retell a clear anecdote with background, event and outcome; consolidate imperfetto and passato prossimo. |
| 2. Explain what happened earlier | Reconstruct a timeline; introduce trapassato after the simpler past contrasts are established. |
| 3. Describe how life changed | Compare former routines and present circumstances; reuse frequency, duration, description and comparison. |
| 4. Give and request things | Use direct and indirect pronouns, then combinations, in practical exchanges; model each cluster before production. |
| 5. Talk about places and quantities | Develop ci and ne in shopping, travel and reference; teach ce ne and any needed participle agreement separately. |
| 6. Identify the right person or place | Clarify a description using che, then preposition plus cui; teach the governing construction. |
| 7. Make a workable plan | Negotiate arrangements and realistic se conditions using familiar present, future, conditional and commands. |
| 8. Explain a problem | Connect cause, purpose, result and an obstacle in a message or call; choose links by intended meaning. |
| 9. Express hopes and doubts | Understand and use frequent subjunctive frames with explicitly introduced forms; avoid untaught paradigm production. |
| 10. Relay a message | Report statements and embedded questions, tracking speaker, person, place and time. |
| 11. Understand rules and write back | Interpret common notices with impersonal si and familiar passives; write a request or explanation. |
| 12. Put B1 together | Hear an announcement, read a related message, explain a change and produce a connected response. |

B2 develops productive control of viewpoint and more sustained argument. Present and imperfect subjunctive forms are taught before counterfactual tasks demand them. Common compound patterns are introduced with worked timelines; C1 revisits their more demanding combinations and stylistic uses. Receptive knowledge of narrative forms precedes unsupported production of unfamiliar irregulars.

| B2 unit | Purpose and language work |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Distinguish fact from stance | Compare fact, opinion, doubt and evaluation; build productive present subjunctive forms and selection. |
| 2. Track time within a viewpoint | Build imperfect and common compound subjunctives; distinguish simultaneous, earlier and later events in explicit contexts. |
| 3. Compare real and unreal options | Discuss present and past counterfactuals with the appropriate conditional results; bridge every new form first. |
| 4. Report an interview or meeting | Relay statements, questions and instructions, adjusting person, tense and time expressions. |
| 5. Explain institutional notices | Interpret and write passives and si passivante; distinguish agreement, agency and impersonal meaning. |
| 6. Keep references precise | Use relatives and resumptive pronouns across several sentences, avoiding ambiguous reference. |
| 7. Condense a sequence safely | Teach infinitive and simple gerund clauses, then needed compound forms, retaining actor and temporal relation. |
| 8. Compare evidence and alternatives | Read an article and simple chart; present advantages, disadvantages, evidence and a qualified conclusion. |
| 9. Respond to an objection | Follow a discussion, concede a point, clarify disagreement and preserve the main claim. |
| 10. Negotiate and correspond | Write a complaint, response or proposal; distinguish courtesy, distance and uncertainty. |
| 11. Read news and narrative | Contrast current reporting with narrative register; recognize passato remoto and common forms before production. |
| 12. Relay useful information | Summarize an interview or document for someone's practical need; separate fact and recommendation. |
| 13. Put B2 together | Compare two sources, write a supported position and respond to an objection in an extended exchange. |

C1 increases the complexity of discourse, implied meaning, precision and adaptation to audience. Familiar grammar returns with a greater communicative demand. Longer reading and listening must be present, even though their practice is divided into manageable stages.

| C1 unit | Purpose and language work |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Attribute a claim accurately | Distinguish evidence, inference, opinion and unconfirmed report; teach reportative conditional and epistemic future. |
| 2. Control complex timelines | Maintain time relationships through reported speech and subordination, including past and pluperfect subjunctive. |
| 3. Build an argument | Organize claim, evidence, qualification, counterargument and conclusion across paragraphs. |
| 4. Concede precisely | Control nuanced concession, cause and consequence without changing the force of the main claim. |
| 5. Manage focus and reference | Resolve antecedents; choose topic or cleft focus; maintain clitics, agreement and cohesion. |
| 6. Compress and expand prose | Use nominalization and nonfinite clauses while preserving actor, time and causality. |
| 7. Choose precise language | Develop collocation, paraphrase, idiom and connotation in context. |
| 8. Follow extended interviews | Identify implied attitude, digression and speaker intention in varied, less scripted audio. |
| 9. Manage discussion | Enter, hold, yield and regain a turn; clarify misunderstanding and address complex objections. |
| 10. Write for an institution | Produce a report, formal letter or commentary with purposeful paragraphing and appropriate tone. |
| 11. Synthesize sources | Compare a written and recorded account, attribute positions and brief a different audience accurately. |
| 12. Put C1 together | Read and hear substantial sources, write a reasoned brief, present it and respond to follow-up. |

C2 increases precision, interpretive depth and flexibility, not just the rarity of the grammatical form. A familiar conditional can require C2-level judgment when tone, implication, competing evidence and a changing audience matter. Regional variation is interpreted in context without prescribing one accent as the standard of success. Specialized topics supply essential background so that an exercise assesses Italian rather than unrelated professional expertise.

| C2 unit | Purpose and language work |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Hear the subtext | Interpret implication, irony, interpersonal stance and changing direction in natural-speed conversation. |
| 2. Reconstruct competing accounts | Combine several spoken and written sources while preserving omissions, uncertainty and conflicting claims. |
| 3. Control degrees of commitment | Handle negation scope, modality, evidential distance and hypothetical framing with exact meaning. |
| 4. Remove consequential ambiguity | Edit difficult reference, agency, timeline and logical relationships across a whole text. |
| 5. Choose lexical nuance | Distinguish near-synonyms, metaphor, idioms, connotations and collocations for a stated audience. |
| 6. Navigate social variation | Interpret colloquial and regional cues; adapt register without treating every spoken variation as an error. |
| 7. Repair delicate interaction | Disagree, mediate tension and resolve pragmatic or cultural misunderstanding. |
| 8. Edit complex prose | Improve information order, parallelism, compression and emphasis while retaining necessary facts. |
| 9. Transfer between genres | Recast evidence as a public explanation, professional brief and informal message, changing detail and tone appropriately. |
| 10. Read literary and historical style | Interpret narrative tense, marked order and stylistic effects; distinguish recognition from ordinary conversational use. |
| 11. Explain specialist information | Make a demanding text accessible to a nonspecialist while preserving qualifications and uncertainty. |
| 12. Put C2 together | Synthesize multiple genres and speakers, produce work for two audiences and handle unscripted follow-up discussion. |

Three continuing pathways make the sequence cumulative: simple past contrast → connected narrative → earlier-past timeline → reported viewpoint → complex temporal/source control; basic object pronouns → combinations → cross-sentence reference → subtle ambiguity repair; realistic conditions → counterfactuals → nuanced commitment and implication. Each unit's authoring brief names earlier targets to retrieve and future units that will reuse them. A shared situation can develop from explaining a missed appointment to comparing remedies, evaluating competing accounts and briefing different audiences.

Cross-cutting coverage includes spelling and accents, consonant length, stress and intonation, article and noun forms, agreement, pronoun systems, negation, question formation, prepositions and governed constructions, tense/aspect/mood, nonfinite forms, word order, cohesion, register, collocation, idiom, interaction strategies, and pragmatic interpretation. Rare or literary constructions receive justified receptive or productive goals. These topics cannot be declared covered merely because they appear incidentally in an example.

Each level also spans multiple lexical domains: personal life, relationships, home, food, travel, services, study/work, leisure and culture, followed by public issues, institutions, media, argument and specialist communication. The inventory records which senses are taught, used productively, revisited and linked to the dictionary. It will not impose an unsupported universal word-count threshold for a CEFR level.

Every unit includes coherent reading input, a reviewed listening sample, a practical response task, and later retrieval, distributed across its short lessons. Beginners receive short, clear exchanges with meaning supplied; later units grow in length, pace, number of speakers, implicit meaning and source complexity. A1 starts with words, signs, forms and short messages; A2 adds routine exchanges and brief accounts; B1 adds connected narratives and practical correspondence; B2 adds arguments and extended exchanges; C1/C2 include substantial texts, varied voices and synthesis. The asset inventory identifies where each kind of input is actually delivered. More difficult vocabulary alone does not make a task advanced.

Each level's checkpoint combines these strands rather than averaging away a major gap. A beginner check might combine a familiar sign, an audio exchange and a short response. An advanced check includes extended comprehension, a written product and an oral or interactive product with separate rubrics. The rubric covers task fulfilment, meaning, accuracy, range, coherence and audience; spoken work adds intelligibility and interaction. Scores, if introduced, must be calibrated against reviewed samples. Self-assessment, automatic checks and independent assessment remain visibly distinct evidence sources.

The level-exit evidence contract is as follows. These are course checkpoints to inform readiness, not accredited examinations. Each task needs fresh content within its taught domain, and evidence from more than one occasion informs retention.

| Stage | Listening and reading | Written use | Spoken use and interaction | Relaying or explaining information |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Foundations | Understand a modelled greeting and match familiar words/phrases to meaning. | Complete a supplied name or phrase frame. | Practise a supported introduction and a request for repetition. | Match a familiar message to its intended meaning. |
| A1 | Recover simple details from a clear short exchange, sign or message. | Produce a short personal message using introduced patterns. | Give an introduction and take part in a supported practical exchange. | Pass on a simple taught detail such as a meeting place. |
| A2 | Understand routine instructions, arrangements and a brief account. | Write a short connected account, invitation or change of plan. | Arrange an activity, describe an event and respond to a routine change. | Relay useful details from a simple notice or conversation. |
| B1 | Follow the main points and relevant details of familiar connected input. | Explain an experience or problem and give reasons in a connected response. | Retell an event, explain a practical problem and handle clarification. | Summarize a practical message for someone who needs to act on it. |
| B2 | Interpret more complex input, distinguish positions and compare evidence. | Write a supported proposal, argument or response to an objection. | Negotiate an option and sustain a viewpoint while responding to questions. | Compare two sources and explain their practical implications. |

Automatic evidence is limited to validated comprehension items, constrained constructions and other bounded decisions. Open writing and oral products are saved as practice/portfolio work until assessed against the rubric; a self-review is stored as self-review. Independent evidence for unscripted interaction requires a real interlocutor and an appropriately qualified assessor or an independently validated assessment capability. A recording of a rehearsed monologue cannot substitute for it. If that assessment is unavailable, learners continue the course with that part of the profile explicitly unassessed. The app must never convert submission, completion of a scripted branch, or optional tutor access into verified C1/C2 interaction.

**The first lessons will make no assumptions about Italian vocabulary or grammar terminology.** Default entry is “Start from the beginning,” with optional placement for people who already know some Italian. Existing users keep their progress and can choose a new starting point. Placement samples several contexts and skills, uses an honest “I don't know,” and routes to a suitable start; it does not manufacture successful attempts or mark every skipped case learned.

The opening uses fully translated, playable expressions. A useful phrase may first be taught as a complete chunk, then unpacked later. Recognizing mi chiamo does not demonstrate knowledge of all reflexive verbs. Grammar words such as subject, infinitive, gender, and tense receive a short explanation at their first useful appearance. Pronunciation is introduced through known words, with listening and visual support. There is no mandatory abstract alphabet examination before the learner can use a greeting.

The first twelve lessons below illustrate the intended pace. Very small greeting steps are combined so they do not become padded lessons. Learners who need repetition can stay with a pattern; those who demonstrate it can move forward. These are authored lesson briefs, with final screens and wording to be designed in the representative-lesson phase.

| Lesson | What is introduced | First practice and boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Hello and goodbye | Ciao, buongiorno and arrivederci, with meaning, audio and a visible social setting. | Match greeting/leave-taking and choose a response; no verb or spelling test. |
| 2. Exchange names | Mi chiamo… and Come ti chiami? as complete expressions in a familiar exchange. | Match question to response, then construct the already-modelled name phrase with supplied names. |
| 3. Meet someone politely | Come si chiama? and the role of polite Lei, compared with the familiar question. | Choose according to the stated relationship; no general reflexive or full third-person paradigm test. |
| 4. Courtesy in a short exchange | Grazie, prego, scusi and per favore in separately modelled situations. | Choose a suitable response or expression; do not treat them as interchangeable politeness words. |
| 5. Ask for help | Non capisco and Può ripetere, per favore? as useful complete expressions. | Hear a short exchange and select the repair; no claim of general potere mastery. |
| 6. Say where you are from | Sono di Roma and another clearly identified place; introduce the meaning of sono in this frame. | Use supplied place names; explain the chosen frame without testing general preposition selection. |
| 7. Talk about yourself and someone else | Sono/sei/è, stated conversational roles, and formal Lei è; introduce one contrast at a time. | Choose and complete forms with familiar names and origin phrases; productive evidence is limited to the taught forms. |
| 8. Meet familiar objects | A small set such as il libro, la casa, il pane and il caffè, with clear senses, meaning and audio. | Match words and meanings; article/gender/plural details are available without testing every form. |
| 9. Name one thing | Il/la and un/una through the already-introduced libro and casa. | Choose the intended naming pattern in modelled contexts; defer lo/uno and plural articles to explicit later lessons. |
| 10. Say what you have | Ho/hai/ha with familiar objects and supplied noun phrases. | Match person and form, then complete a taught construction; age and hunger wait for their own explanation. |
| 11. Hear a spelling contrast | A small c sound contrast using familiar words plus cena only after its meaning and sound are introduced. | Connect heard sound and spelling. Teach the silent-i cue explicitly if using ciao; no generic sentence-order task counts for phonics. |
| 12. Put the first exchange together | Reuse greetings, name, origin, courtesy and familiar possession phrases. | Understand a short dialogue and construct a response with support fading; new numbers and age are introduced in the next appropriate sequence. |

Numbers are taught in small useful groups before phone details, prices, time or age tasks require them. A learner must not be asked to state an adult age after learning only one to ten. Personal responses can use fictional people; the course does not require disclosure of age, address, or other private details.

**A lesson will have an authored sequence that gradually releases support.** The following is a design pattern, not a rigid quota. A simple contrast may take one cycle; a more complex construction may require several short cycles within the unit.

| Moment | Learner experience | What it can establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Orient | A short practical goal and an understandable situation. | Purpose, without scoring. |
| Prepare | Necessary new words or chunks, with meaning and audio; refresh only what is needed. | Exposure and supported recognition. |
| Model | One explanation and a worked example; highlight the part that changes. | Introduction of the target. |
| Try together | A partially solved task, an available cue, or a small guided contrast. | Supported performance. |
| Practise | Varied activities with progressively less support and controlled background vocabulary. | Evidence about the specific skill assessed. |
| Apply | A fresh, meaningful context using introduced material. | Transfer, without mistaking unseen vocabulary for the target. |
| Close | A practical recap, saved progress, and the choice to continue, return, or learn related vocabulary. | Lesson completion for covered targets. |
| Revisit | A later task, selected from current needs. | Retention beyond the immediate teaching context. |

Explicit models, manageable information, and a gradual increase in independent work follow the principles in the [NSW cognitive load practice guide](https://education.nsw.gov.au/about-us/education-data-and-research/cese/publications/practical-guides-for-educators/cognitive-load-theory-in-practice). Retrieval and spacing will be planned across lessons, consistent with the [IES instruction and study practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1). Exact lesson lengths and evidence policies will be piloted in Parola; those sources do not validate a universal answer count for this app.

For pacing, use short explanations, usually one rule or contrast per screen, and a small active set of words. A provisional beginner target is roughly three to five new lexical items in a short lesson, reduced when the structure is demanding. That is an authoring constraint to test, not a CEFR vocabulary quota. Most lessons should fit a short sitting, with natural save points. Longer reading, listening, and writing tasks at higher levels are divided into meaningful stages while retaining access to the whole text.

**Exercise formats will be chosen for the skill they reveal.** Matching connects meanings, referents, persons, or forms. Multiple choice distinguishes a taught contrast. Cloze tests a specific missing component with surrounding language supplied. Letter tiles support spelling; they do not demonstrate unsupported spelling recall. Ordering tests syntax or discourse organization only when the model answer is no longer displayed. Short writing tests production when the learner has the relevant words and forms. Dialogue responses test communicative appropriateness. Listening tasks test information recovered from audio. Advanced editing and synthesis test how language preserves meaning across a passage.

A picture or visual scene should help establish meaning, not become a second puzzle. Distractors should represent plausible taught confusions. A deliberately misspelled choice belongs in an explicitly labelled spelling activity; arbitrary malformed sentences should not be presented as natural models. Vary activity format without switching to unrelated concepts just to create variety or satisfy a repetition counter.

As an acceptance example, the current chilo exercise becomes a small sound–spelling sequence: introduce il chilo and its meaning and sound; model the relevant c/ch distinction; try a supported spelling decision; retrieve the pattern later with another introduced word; then apply it in the bread-shopping sentence. The other sound patterns have their own introduction and evidence. Ordering Il gelato è buono cannot count as evidence for hearing or spelling g.

**Vocabulary will be integrated at the point where it is needed.** Each occurrence refers to the intended sense, not merely a headword. Store exposure, supported understanding, independent recognition, and productive recall separately where relevant. Course completion, dictionary completion, and explicit manual completion remain different records.

Nouns are introduced with the appropriate article, gender, and supported plural information. The details panel always offers these when applicable, including invariant, mass, plural-only, and sense-dependent forms; an introductory card need not test all of them at once. The calcio example must keep football and individual kicks distinct. Verbs expose the infinitive, contextual form, person, tense, construction, meaning, and relevant exception. Multiword expressions retain their combined meaning alongside individual word lookup.

Every visible Italian sentence remains tappable word by word, including inflected verbs. English meaning and Italian sentence meaning must agree. A lookup of a background noun can remain compatible with evidence for a tense choice; a lookup revealing the missing target form makes that answer supported. The same distinction applies to conjugation tables, translations, hints, and transcripts. Revealing a transcript may support reading evidence, but cannot be called independent listening evidence.

The default combined session prepares any necessary words inside the main lesson, then offers deeper related word or selected verb-case practice. It does not force a complete conjugation lesson before a learner can understand one grammar example. Familiar lexical anchors recur across units; new contexts gradually broaden them. An unrelated random word must not be substituted just to fill a session slot.

**Grammar explanations will separate meaning, form, and use.** Teach what the construction communicates, how it is formed, and when it is appropriate. Contrast alternatives when the learner has enough background. Regular rules are explicitly described as patterns with exceptions. Before a participle check, teach the selected verb's actual form and auxiliary: -are → -ato, -ere → -uto, and -ire → -ito are patterns; dire → detto and other exceptions are introduced before assessment. Progressive explanations teach -ando/-endo and relevant exceptions without mechanically applying stare to every verb sense.

The past sequence revisits the user's requested distinction in coherent situations: sto mangiando focuses on now; stavo mangiando on an action in progress at a past moment; mangiavo can describe a past habit or an ongoing/background situation; ho mangiato presents a bounded completed event in the relevant context. These are starting contrasts, followed by more nuanced examples. Temporal words alone must not be treated as infallible tense selectors. A future exercise that specifically requests the future must explain that requirement without declaring a natural present-tense alternative universally ungrammatical.

Formal Lei is taught alongside tu and third-person forms, with explicit conversational roles. Capitalization is not the sole cue. A learner's gender is not guessed for participle or adjective agreement; prompts either provide a referent or accept the appropriate alternatives. Grammar examples must respect real verb usage, collocations, prepositions, argument structure, and register.

**Adaptation will be based on the target and the observed answer.** Use distinct skills for meaning, form selection, form production, agreement, sound–spelling mapping, word order, reference, register, interpretation, and discourse organization. Each question names the primary target and any supplied components. An event records the response, support used, context, modality, feedback exposure, and eligible evidence. Related skills can receive credit only when the activity genuinely assesses them.

| Observed difficulty | Response | Subsequent evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unknown word or instruction | Explain meaning or task; keep the grammar familiar. | Retry the intended target without treating vocabulary uncertainty as a diagnosed grammar error. |
| Confused contrast | Show a concise side-by-side model and a discriminating example. | A fresh choice or construction that distinguishes the alternatives. |
| Correct meaning, wrong form | Preserve what was correct; isolate the relevant ending, auxiliary, agreement, or placement. | Return to the complete construction after component support. |
| Repeated trouble producing a known form | Provide a partial answer or smaller step. | Fade support and revisit later. |
| Sound or listening difficulty | Replay with appropriate support; teach the contrast using known language. | Fresh audio evidence; text-supported work remains labelled by what it assessed. |
| Valid but unexpected response | Accept verified alternatives or route to an ungraded comparison/report path. | Do not invent a misconception or penalize a correct response. |
| Continued difficulty or fatigue | Offer another explanation, a smaller target, pause, or explicit skip. | Preserve weak targets for return without an endless identical loop. |

One wrong choice creates a tentative explanation, not a confident diagnosis. Where two causes are plausible, the next small activity distinguishes them. Hints are graduated: orient to meaning, point to the relevant cue, offer a partial model, then reveal and explain. Repairs stay with the current target and introduced vocabulary. They must not advance to an unrelated authored question after merely repeating the same general rule.

**Progress will distinguish coverage, independent performance, and retention.** The internal states are introduced, supported, independent, demonstrated in transfer, and retained through later retrieval. These are not labels to burden the lesson interface. Completion requires evidence for every essential taught target in that lesson; recognition alone cannot clear a productive goal, and one spelling pattern cannot clear all others. A single correct guess is never sufficient for broad mastery.

The evidence contract for each target specifies meaningful variation, the task types that can assess it, and the conditions for fresh independent work. Initial implementation uses inspectable rules and conservative, configurable thresholds. It does not introduce an uncalibrated probability score presented as scientific certainty. The current full verb-case coverage contract is preserved. Grammar policies are designed per target family and evaluated against learner outcomes, rather than replacing the old universal two-success rule with another universal number.

Later review selects weak or due targets across contexts, not always question one. Successful supported practice does not manufacture a delayed retention result. A lapse affects confidence and scheduling, while historical lesson completion remains visible. Incorrect work does not erase evidence for unrelated skills. Manual word/case completion is respected as the user's declaration and enrolls the appropriate diagnostic review without fabricating correct attempts. Unchecking a case removes its automatic case-review enrollment; independent grammar learning remains separate.

**Assessment will grow with the learner's ability.** Each unit ends with a short practical task that combines its taught material. Periodic checkpoints revisit prior units with fresh examples, including receptive and productive work. Level checkpoints use multiple tasks and a skill profile; there is no single multiple-choice pass score labelled “C2.” Learners can still explore the next level, with a clear recommendation for unfinished foundations.

For constrained answers, normalize harmless typography and accept all reviewed valid forms that satisfy the prompt. Do not erase distinctions being taught, such as è versus e. Where several word orders or synonyms are natural, either accept them or state a genuinely useful constraint before the task. An editorial semantic-preservation check is mandatory for rewrites, including negation, time, agency, modality, quantity, and conditions such as only if. Automated checks of meaning are confined to tightly constrained transformations with independently reviewed alternatives; this is not a general offline semantic assessor. Tests include legitimate alternatives authored independently of the answer key.

The engine cannot reliably recognize every plausible free response with an answer list. Automatic diagnosis is limited to reviewed alternatives and known error patterns within a bounded task. Unrecognized open responses move to model comparison, self-review or a narrower follow-up; they are not assigned a confident linguistic error merely because their string is absent. A learner can flag an answer for editorial review without having to accept an incorrect explanation. This reporting path must be usable locally and must not silently send the learner's writing elsewhere.

Open writing and speaking require models, explanation, a rubric, revision, and comparison. Default operation remains offline and does not require a paid model or cloud account. Local recording and playback are optional when supported and permitted; learners can practise aloud without recording. A bounded pronunciation or transcription service, if later added, cannot be treated as a definitive assessor of communicative proficiency. Uncertain open work is not falsely marked wrong. A portfolio and optional tutor/qualified reviewer feedback support broader readiness judgments. Advanced tasks remain part of the course even where feedback is reflective rather than automatically scored.

Authored branching dialogues provide supported interaction practice. Unscripted interaction needs a real interlocutor or a separately evaluated conversational capability; selecting a scripted response cannot prove that skill. The default course supplies partner/tutor task prompts and oral reflection alongside independent practice, without requiring a paid service. Recordings remain local unless the learner explicitly exports them. Audio unavailability or a decision to skip speaking leaves that modality unassessed while allowing the course to continue.

Pronunciation aims at understandable speech, sound contrasts, stress, rhythm, and intonation. It does not require erasing a learner's accent. This follows the emphasis on intelligibility in the [Council of Europe's phonological competence guidance](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/phonological-competence). Audio should include reviewed natural speech, gradually increasing pace and speaker variety. Browser TTS may remain useful for simple replay but is insufficient as the sole advanced listening source. Original or appropriately licensed recordings/texts need provenance and a download plan.

**The visual design will preserve the immersive experience while making the learning sequence clear.** The lesson title and practical goal lead into one active activity. A compact progress treatment describes meaningful stages and covered targets; repair work should not make a fake question quota run backwards. Keep the top information dropdown beside EN, read-only Back within lesson history, Pause, sentence lookup, and the established feedback panel. Answers grade on selection where appropriate; feedback waits for Continue. Written submission still uses an explicit action or Enter.

Matching cards, focused contrast cards, disappearing pairs, sentence construction, example carousels, short dialogues, and passage annotations reuse established components from Words, Play, articles/gender, and conjugation drills. Necessary controls remain visible; optional explanations expand where they are needed. The primary action stays visible at the bottom; longer content scrolls inside the intended content area. Carousels are centered and keyboard-accessible, with clear alternatives to swiping. Avoid nested invisible borders, redundant progress bars, overlapping controls, or decorative dead space.

Early explanations use concise English with Italian examples; Italian instructions and explanations become more prominent as comprehension grows, while EN remains available. Revealing translation changes only evidence for a task that actually depended on understanding the untranslated material. Successful word and verb answers retain the existing Italian playback behavior; long-passage tasks offer a purposeful replay control instead of unexpectedly speaking an entire essay.

The full-height shell must be tested in Safari and an installed iPhone PWA, including the user's iPhone 16 Pro Max and reported iOS 26.6.1, keyboard opening, orientation, dynamic text, safe areas, and history navigation. Also cover smaller phones, landscape, desktop, reduced motion, screen readers, visible focus, and non-color feedback. Muted or text-supported access must remain useful while recording honestly which modality was practised.

**The implementation will replace inferred ordering with explicit authored steps.** Extend the existing grammar curriculum and runtime rather than broadly rewriting the app. A versioned content contract will describe step IDs, targets, required knowledge, vocabulary occurrences, models, assistance, response rules, repair branches, completion requirements, and later-review variants. The cursor stores the actual step and branch, answer draft, shuffled controls, support, and history. The sequence must no longer depend on the arbitrary formula first card → first question → second card → question rotation.

Content, interaction rendering, evidence evaluation, review selection, and course recommendation remain separate modules. The existing learning model, store, sentence lookup, completion controls, and SRS integrations are reused through explicit interfaces. Runtime content remains authored and validated; production lessons do not require live sentence generation. Assets use manifests and versioned content packs. Large audio collections should be downloadable by unit or level rather than making installation download the entire C2 library. A silent fallback must not turn an unavailable listening assessment into claimed listening success.

**Existing progress will survive the transition without invented mastery.** Preserve raw events, manual word and case records, stars, custom entries, XP, reset boundaries, backups, and cloud merges. Maintain an explicit old-to-new objective mapping. Transfer specific valid evidence only where the target is genuinely equivalent. Broad historical completion remains acknowledged, while newly separated skills can receive a short refresher or diagnostic rather than a forced full replay or automatic pass.

The migration decision is to retain the v1 content registry and compatible renderer for unfinished lessons and existing due reviews. Resume continues the saved v1 session by default; the learner may explicitly choose the new course path through a mapped checkpoint or diagnostic. V2 target IDs and session keys include a separate curriculum namespace/version, preventing collision with existing g: lesson sessions. A v1 cursor never reads a v2 question bank. Legacy due items remain reachable until a documented equivalent replaces them or a diagnostic establishes the relevant new targets; merged scheduling prevents duplicate prompts for the same learning need. Old broad readiness cannot grant every newly separated target.

Back navigation and repeated taps cannot duplicate attempts or rewards. Migrations are idempotent, survive offline merging, and protect newer data from older clients. Rollback preserves new progress even if an old client cannot interpret it. Updates activate only after a complete compatible shell is cached; failed audio downloads do not corrupt the working app. Compatibility removal requires a later explicit migration with preserved history and review mappings, rather than simply deleting the old packs.

**Delivery will proceed through bounded releases with a complete curriculum map from the start.** Foundations through C2 remain the committed scope. Incremental implementation is a quality strategy: an early release must not be presented as completing the whole redesign, and unfinished levels must not receive completion claims merely because their outlines exist. Every existing lesson receives a retain, split, rewrite, move, or retire decision with a progress mapping.

The requested execution arrangement is one central orchestrator on GPT-6 Astra with ultra reasoning, with directed subagents on GPT-6 Sol with ultra reasoning. Use up to three workers concurrently within the available four-agent capacity. The orchestrator owns the shared contract, curriculum coherence, integration, acceptance decisions, and eventual release. Subagents own bounded deliverables and report evidence; they do not independently publish changes.

Future implementation ownership will be assigned by file boundary. Content workers own separate level/unit packs and their review notes. A runtime worker owns grammar journey/evidence modules; a UI worker owns the grammar activity components and associated styles; a validation worker owns independent fixtures and tests. Shared files such as the learning schema, store, application routes, service worker, and course integration have one owner at a time, normally the orchestrator. Interface changes are agreed before dependent edits. Independent tool reads and checks run concurrently; dependent migrations, integration, and deployment run sequentially.

Validation authorship and linguistic signoff are tracked separately. A worker may implement a schema validator and later author content, but cannot supply the sole expected-answer oracle or approval for that content. Root or a different reviewer owns C1/C2 answer fixtures and signoff when Worker C writes those lessons. Content authors cannot amend their own acceptance criteria to make a failing lesson pass. Cross-review findings are resolved before root integrates each unit.

The current application is a static JavaScript PWA, so the plan avoids introducing a new framework or mandatory backend. The new grammar evidence policy needs an explicit versioned parser and migration: the current normalizer recognizes grammar-v1, and a new policy must not fall through to ordinary word evidence. Content and stored evidence versions are separate, with compatibility tests for both. A content author cannot solve a validation failure by weakening the shared evidence policy or deleting a legitimate alternate answer.

The final scope includes a complete curriculum map, a decision on every existing lesson, all replacement lesson and review content, lexical/sense dependencies, audio/text assets, the lesson player and adaptive engine, level-appropriate production tasks, progress migration, independent validation, and release documentation. Delivery estimates come after the target inventory and representative units establish authoring and review cost. Expect hundreds of short lessons across the whole course; the count will follow coverage and practice needs, not a promise that an arbitrary lesson total produces C2 proficiency.

**Release acceptance will check pedagogy as well as software behavior.** These are required checks for each new unit and engine change:

1. Every assessed target has an earlier introduction or an explicit valid prerequisite on every reachable path. Background vocabulary is familiar or supplied. Correct answers are not visible during an independent check.
2. Every objective has aligned tasks, meaningful variants, a repair path, a transfer opportunity, and later review. No lesson can finish while an essential target is never assessed.
3. Italian forms, collocations, naturalness, translations, register, exceptions, and distractors receive an independent content review. Advanced rewrites preserve meaning and admit legitimate alternatives.
4. Simulated learners cover all-correct, guessing, repeated misconception, unknown vocabulary, supported success, valid alternate, skip, late return, and insufficient content. The simulation's oracle must not simply copy the authored answer key.
5. A reviewer unfamiliar with the lesson follows it in its actual rendered order, stating what a zero-knowledge learner has been taught at each point. Representative learner walkthroughs are used to refine pacing; if external learners are unavailable, simulated reviews are labelled as such.
6. Mastery and review checks distinguish same-session practice, later retention, modality, support, manual completion, and unlearned cases. Word lookup and transcript support affect only the relevant evidence.
7. Mobile and desktop interaction tests verify feedback, visible Continue, accessible alternatives, safe areas, scrolling, history, and audio behavior. Browser emulation is not reported as physical iPhone verification.
8. Persistence tests start with real-shaped legacy fixtures and cover install, upgrade, offline resume, two-device merges, backup/restore, resets, and rollback protection.
9. Published verification checks the exact deployed commit, matching content/asset versions, successful Pages deployment, and service-worker upgrade with existing progress. No reinstall or cache deletion is a substitute for an update that preserves learning data.

**Success will be measured by understandable progress, not question volume.** The evaluation plan records prerequisite failures, invalid-answer reports, repeated repair loops, ability to use a pattern in a fresh context, later retrieval, and completion without help where that evidence is appropriate. Speed and streaks are not proxies for language ability. Learning-event analysis is local by default; external analytics or research data collection would be a separate product decision. Course percentages indicate completed course work, with separate information about skills needing practice.

Before implementation, the proposal's main decisions are the practical unit sequence, the lesson and evidence contract, and the expanded listening/production plan. Detailed lesson wording and audio sourcing then proceed through the same review standard. No external contributor has been commissioned, and no paid service is assumed by this plan.
